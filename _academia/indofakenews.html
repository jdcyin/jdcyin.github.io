Background

Why this matters

Disinformation shaped Indonesia's 2017 Jakarta governor election and its 2019 presidential election, and has long fed violence and discrimination against minorities.

Yet little research asks who fake news hurts most.

Background

A country of minorities

Most Indonesians are Sunni Muslim. Christians, Hindus, Shia Muslims and Chinese Indonesians are small communities, with a long history of being targeted in ethno-religious tensions.

Background

What we asked

Which campaigns target minorities, how they change minorities' behaviour, and whether the state helps or makes things worse.

Results

Everyone had heard the hoaxes

Every participant had encountered fake news. It spiked around elections and disasters, and played on existing religious and ethnic fears.

Results

Lying low to stay safe

The harms included psychological stress, economic damage, and sexual and physical violence. Many minorities responded by keeping a low profile, staying quiet or, for some Hindus, converting.

Results

A spiral of fear and silence

For women and LGBT people, fake news fuels fear, violence and self-censorship. Silence then emboldens the perpetrators.

Results

Who spreads it, and why

Participants saw politicians and religious figures using fake news to win votes and build religious standing, each outbidding the other in hardline appeals.

Results

A state that is part of the problem

Most participants felt the government's response was inadequate, and that policing of fake news was biased and politicised.

Discussion

A tool of social control

Fake news lets dominant groups discipline minorities. By pushing them to self-censor and conform, it normalizes discrimination without the need for force.

Discussion

Fixing it from both ends

Stronger regulation of social media platforms can curb the worst effects, but ground-up education on fake news and how it is used politically matters just as much.

Methods

How we did it

We interviewed 24 Indonesians, four from each of six minority communities, recruited through personal networks and NGOs. More than half were women.

Each author coded the transcripts separately before reconciling the results. As a qualitative study, it captures lived experience rather than measuring effects statistically.
